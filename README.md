# Brick Commons

Brick Commons begins with a childhood memory: sitting around LEGO bricks with friends, and sometimes strangers, discovering what we could make together. The finished model mattered, but so did the suggestions, disagreements, and small decisions that made it ours. This browser-based building space brings that experience into a shared digital setting.

For me, “good” means establishing a clear expressive intention and carrying it through the whole work. Visuals, navigation, rules, and features should reinforce one another. In Brick Commons, that intention is returning to the experience of gathering around bricks, building together, and keeping something from that time.

## A table before a menu

The homepage makes this intention tangible. A parts tray, noticeboard, sign, booklet, and creation shelf become functional objects on a building table. Selecting an object moves the camera towards it, and suitable interactions happen there. Navigation becomes part of entering and using the space.

Henry Jenkins’s [*Game Design as Narrative Architecture*](https://electronicbookreview.com/publications/game-design-as-narrative-architecture/) explains how designed spaces can establish narrative possibilities without prescribing a plot. This provides a vocabulary for understanding the table: its arrangement and interactions establish a shared activity before construction begins.

Beach Houses, Medieval Castle, and Woodland Commons extend that invitation into different imaginative settings, supporting two, four, and six builders respectively. Reference buildings offer a starting point, while players determine what belongs in their own creation. There is no required resemblance or completion score.

## Social interaction with a purpose

Social connection belongs to this experience, but every social feature serves building. Friends and invitations help people gather. Shared editing lets them change the same construction. Plan mode places transparent proposals directly where they would be built, and marks draw attention to ideas.

There is no built-in text or voice chat. This is a deliberate boundary: I want the application’s communication tools to remain centred on the work taking shape. Players can propose a roof, respond to a window arrangement, or turn another person’s plan into real construction.

Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek’s [MDA framework](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf) connects rules, behaviour during play, and the experience produced through them. It helps articulate my design standard: shared inventory and planning tools belong because they support this particular experience. A larger feature list would not make the expression stronger.

## Keeping what we made

Saved versions, contributor credits, collections, and independent continuations carry the experience beyond a session. Participants can retain a shared creation and return to it without overwriting the saved version. Credits acknowledge participation without turning brick counts into a competition.

Survival systems, physical collapse, competitive rankings, and runtime AI generation are excluded. Each would introduce priorities outside the experience I chose to build.

Visitors can start as guests: choose a scene from the table to build alone or create a room, or use the noticeboard to join others. Accounts allow the same identity to return across devices.

## One intention throughout

Automated checks enforce inventory, construction, access, and persistence rules. In my live multiplayer test, operations worked and shared changes appeared effectively immediately. Logs identified the two participants and recorded changes to their shared construction.

My argument for narrative coherence rests on the implementation itself: the table establishes the activity, the environments give it a place, the tools organise collaboration, and saved creations preserve its result. Every part carries the same intention. That consistency—across what players see, do, share, and keep—is what makes Brick Commons good.
